  Broadcast television gave its audience two things at once: they saw the same things, and they knew that everyone else had seen them too. In [The Specification Dilemma](/essays/specification-dilemma/), I argued, in brief,
that sparse prompts to an LLM give most control to the model's priors, and that
because those priors are the same for every user, the outputs converge.
My experiment was to imagine 30 users with distinct intents asking for a blog post.
In each case, two prompts were devised, one sparse and one dense^[We refer you to *The Specification Dilemma* itself, but be assured that there are actual rigorous definitions of these.], and the sparse prompts yielded essentially the same blog post 
30 times, while the dense yielded a meaningful variety. My
principal concern was diversity: a society that fills its gaps from one source
ends up with fewer ideas than it started with, and, in something of a [feedback loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_feedback),
shrinks the pool further and further with time.

The evidence in my work still stands, and I stand by it; if anything, I believe that the six 
months since I wrote the piece have broadly demonstrated the phenomenon I was predicting. Perhaps, then, the key word is *broadly* - I now have formulated some more precise ideas.

Convergence (which we'll define shortly) is hardly new. For a few decades 
in the last century, most of the United States population watched the same evening news on
one of three channels, a convergence beyond any that the advent of LLMs and agents has
yet provided. What broadcast brought along with it, and what the model crucially
does *not* bring back, is the knowledge that such a convergence was even happening in the first place.

Before we continue to explore the difference between those two versions of convergence, 
let's formalize working definitions to be used throughout:

- *Convergence* is similarity of states. Two people converge when they
  end up thinking, writing or wanting the same thing; nobody has to
  notice, and it is not implied whatsoever that anyone does.
- *Common knowledge* is the stronger condition that
  [David Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Lewis_(philosopher))
  and, later, [Michael Chwe](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=5uZ8Mw8AAAAJ&hl=en) placed at the center of how groups coordinate:
  roughly, that everyone knows something, everyone knows that everyone knows it, and so
  on, [without end](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_knowledge_(logic)) [@lewis1969convention; @chwe2001rational].
- *Integration* is what a public does with common knowledge: it
  recognizes what it shares, and so can act on it accordingly. German media
  scholarship calls this the *Integrationsfunktion* of the media; [Habermas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%BCrgen_Habermas)
  would call it an inclusive
  [public sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_sphere).

A shared language model homogenizes through what its users don't notice, through mechanisms like
those I proposed in *The Specification Dilemma*. This brings back a convergence not dissimilar to
that of broadcast, one that will in all likelihood even surpass it in breadth, *without* the
common knowledge that broadcast provided. When LLM-generated text circulates as if a
person wrote it, the convergence hides further, and our attempts to detect it push it deeper still. 

## The Bundle

[Elihu Katz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elihu_Katz), writing in 1996 
as the network audience was being frayed by cable, described television at its height
as "a virtual town meeting" (p. 29)[@katz1996deliver] and "the central civic space" (p. 24)[@katz1996deliver]. The quality of
everyone watching television wasn't enough to make it such a town, no — it was that you could
reasonably expect your "family, neighbors, coworkers, friends, and
enemies" to be watching too (p. 29)[@katz1996deliver].

That statement implies convergence, rather obviously: on a given evening, 
a large share of a country saw the same images, heard the same stories, and
laughed at the same jokes (or, failed to find the same joke attempts funny). It *also* yields us
common knowledge: everyone knew that everyone else had taken
these in, and, more so, knew that the others knew the same. Christina Holtz-Bacha,
describing German television under the public-broadcasting monopoly, said
viewers were more or less forced into experiencing it
together.^[*"Gewissermaßen zum gemeinsamen Erleben gezwungen"*
[@holtzbacha1997fragmentierte]. This is one of those moments where German just seems like the ultimate language: *gemeinsames
Erleben* is experiencing-together, not merely seeing-the-same.]

It is tempting to treat the common knowledge as a by-product of convergence.
Chwe's *Rational Ritual* [@chwe2001rational] illustrates this nicely. A basic example is something
like the following: whether I go
to the protest depends on whether I expect others to go, which itself depends
on whether they expect me to, and so on. I'd argue his best evidence, however, is the [Super Bowl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Bowl_television_ratings). 
Advertisers whose products are worth more paid more per viewer for
the biggest shared broadcasts. Why? Because those broadcasts sold a precious commodity, something no
everyday scattered audience could: that each viewer knew, with high certainty, that
everyone else had seen the same ad. I recognized this the moment I read it. When I was
in middle school and high school, since my [team of choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffalo_Bills) was never relevant when Super Bowl time came around, 
the point of discussion the next day was always which commercials were the coolest. Of course, for us teenagers, the discussion was
heavily skewed towards which brands stereotypical American teenagers would find the most appealing, but I digress.  For Chwe, what makes an event public is *not* its size, but rather its conspicuousness. People see it, and they see each other seeing it, like
a crowd standing in an inward-facing circle. I find his construction quite elegant.

Newer media brought with it a wave of intimacy that did not go unnoticed, and that was the subject of much scrutiny. 
Richard Sennett complained in 1977, for example, that television had reduced
politics to "a dyad": a face on the screen, a viewer watching it in
silence [@sennett1977fall]. Given the state of (US) politics in 2026, perhaps this was a somewhat prescient observation. 
Politics in not only the United States but around the world transitioned into an intimacy everyone knew everyone was having. 
In the years since the country's founding, the landscape changed: where heavily localized elections had far higher turnout than the presidential election
at the turn of the 19th century^[In the year 1800, the [presidential election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1800_United_States_elections) had a voter turnout of about 30%, up from about 6% in 1792; still, local elections had higher turnout, with highly literate states in New England easily surpassing 50% in the early 1800s and jumping towards 70% in some cases.], in the 20th century the president came into every living room at once, whether by fireside chats, television, or, in the 21st... [Twitter?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Use_of_X_by_Donald_Trump), and every living room knew it and still knows it to this day.

At this point, before we continue, it would be worthwhile to step back. George Gerbner's
[cultivation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cultivation_theory)
holds that the lasting effects of television weren't the
conspicuous ones - the episode you remember, the speech you argued about - but slow,
unnoticed frames. The heaviest viewers came to see the world as
[meaner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_world_syndrome) than it was,
drifting toward some shared television outlook without ever deciding to do so [@gerbner1976living].
Broadcast, then, homogenized through what people didn't notice, too. This is generally the strongest objection to the way that I've represented
broadcast thus far; I think it is a valid one. Still, consider that those
quiet frames rode on a loud, shared event. You might not have noticed *what*
the crime shows were teaching you about crime, how [Judge Judy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judge_Judy) and [The People's Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_People%27s_Court) would come to
misinform all of us about how actual legal proceedings work, but you *did* know that everyone
watched crime shows, and, theoretically speaking, anyone could sit down and watch the same hours
of programming if they so desired. That, in fact, is what Gerbner did: his team
coded samples of prime-time programming year after year, and counted. The convergence had an address.

## Small Courts
::: dropcap
The bundle seemed to come apart.
:::

First it was cable, then this internet thing, and eventually the social media feed split
a singular audience into many. Drawing on Katz, it's something analogous to the
childhood game of [king of the hill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_of_the_hill_(game)):
the newspaper knocked the reigning king off of the hill, and radio somehow put him back.
Broadcast restored a kind of representative publicity, a court of national proportions where the ruler
displays himself before the people. Segmentation failed to abolish the court, but it did miniaturize it.
Holtz-Bacha foresaw a multitude of small
sub-publics^[*"Eine Vielzahl kleiner Teilpublika."*] that we came to get:
we find ourselves with a great many small courts to choose from,
each bringing with it its own figure - the streamer, the podcaster, the independent researcher with a blaringly overly grandiose website (yours truly) - and
each with its own increasingly devoted faithful.

Why do I say "increasingly devoted"?  The figures in these courts are intimate in a *new* way.
They answer comments, they read names off the screen, they talk about their lives. danah
boyd calls the result parasocial media [@boyd2026parasocial]. Paolo Gerbaudo describes publics
that cluster rather than gather [@gerbaudo2026tiktok]. Harff and colleagues describe the
proximal mass opinion leader that gave many of the most prominent names of the 21st century 
the charisma that enabled such prominence, that charm of someone who speaks to a mass 
yet feels like someone you know [@harff2026revisiting]. Habermas, returning to the public sphere a staggering sixty years
after he first described its structural transformation, finds a
peculiarly anonymous intimacy (p. 62)[@habermas2022strukturwandel].^[*"Eine eigentümlich anonyme
Intimität."*]

Bennett and Manheim judged fragmentation as much an effect as a cause (p. 221)[@bennett2006onestep],
and Habermas finds fragmentation and the dissolving of the public sphere's edges reinforcing each other
(p. 47)[@habermas2022strukturwandel].^[The next awesome German word is *Entgrenzung*: the public sphere losing its
boundaries, so that anyone can publish and nothing marks where public
speech ends.]

What to make of all this history? I promise I'm going somewhere: fragmentation
didn't unbundle convergence and common knowledge. **It shrank them together.** 
Inside each small court, there is still convergence - the fans have all seen the same video, and
the videos all have the same types of thumbnails, and they're all on the same streaming platform -
and still common knowledge: they know the others have seen it, and the
comment section is where they watch each other watching. Across courts,
there is generally neither. The audience got smaller and the shared knowledge got
smaller, roughly proportionately in rate. Perhaps the worry about fragmentation was really
just a disguised worry about scale.

## The Most Intimate Figure

And to our new toy, the latest king to emerge victorious on the hill for some indeterminate period of time, the language model.
Spoiler alert: convergence comes back, but common knowledge does not.

Let's begin with our previous notion of intimacy. There has never been any figure
from any medium, any court, with anywhere near the level of intimacy as the language model.
The language model, many^[But not all; in an age where even the [Riemann Hypothesis](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/) has been solved by a language model, there are still people with legitimate backgrounds making this argument. I will privately judge them, but not withhold this fact from you, dearest reader.] have realized, is not just a [stochastic parrot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_parrot),
nor just a fancier search engine where the novelty wears off after about 3.14 queries, but rather something that answers specifically to the individual. The nature of that specificity is multi-faceted: it answers you
specifically, addressing the exact problem you have, in your precise terms, and does it on your watch. Want to bang some prompts out at two in the morning? Join the rest of us!  Lou, studying influencers, found their reciprocity to be collective
(pp. 11–12)[@lou2022trans]: simply put, they answer the audience as a whole, and the individual fan
gets only a share of a reply addressed to everyone. In contrast, the model's
reciprocity is individual, and it is individual at the mindblowing scale of
hundreds of millions of distinct conversations at once.

Behind all those individual replies, those mindblowing quantities of conversations and
prompts and ideas, stands one ominous figure. In the earlier
essay, I put it in terms of bits: give your model of choice 10 bits out of
the 10,000 a task needs, and the other 9,990 come from priors 
that are the same for every user. I'm sorry to tell you that personalization does
*not* rescue this. [Naser](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10714031/v1) finds aggregate
homogenization despite personalization (p. 2)[@naser2026sameness]: each reply is fitted to
its asker, and the whole distribution of replies narrows regardless.
Switching model providers also can't rescue us; Naser calls this training
convergence (p. 9)[@naser2026sameness], and Wenger and colleagues found that the outputs of
different models resemble one another more closely than the outputs of
different people do [@wenger2025different].

I've seen the same phenomenon in multiple ways: a distilled
model that reads, sentence by sentence, like the one it learned from.
Models even when *not* distilled from one another still sound more similar to
other models than to humans. I use two persistent [Obsidian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsidian_(software))
vaults for just about everything on my device, one that is written exclusively by
me and one written exclusively by AI agents (with a corpus of roughly 2.7 million words of distinct prose). I did a linguistic
analysis between the agentic vault and my own vault (supplemented with some of my other writings, like
those on this website), even going so far as to create a [Mark V. Shaney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_V._Shaney)-like
system, [`shaney.py`](/essays/convergence-without-integration/shaney.py){.source-ref data-source-path="content/essays/convergence-without-integration/shaney.py"}, trained on each corpus, expecting to show how disjunct they were. They weren't, and I'll come back to that at the end.  Another way I've seen this
is how the labs keep arriving at the same results at roughly the
same time, without coordination and, often, without knowing how close the
others are: convergence without common knowledge, again. I've rigorously written
about this in [The Natural Order Hypothesis](/essays/natural-order-hypothesis/).

So, let's compare and contrast. Broadcast was one source, explicitly known to be one, watched together.
The model is one source, experienced as many disparate sources, consumed alone.
Every conversation feels bespoke because in its details it is. How couldn't it be? Nobody knows
what anyone else asked, after all, nor what they were told in response. It's as if, through a loophole, we're
no longer [*Bowling Alone*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bowling_Alone): we've a hundred million copies of the same
lane and everyone's bowling at the same time in an outward appearance of bowling *together*, yet the lanes are private,
and no one can see the status of the others. The leaderboard above each lane has only one contestant.

## Conspicuous and Inconspicuous

Why *doesn't* this convergence become common knowledge on its own? Enough
people use the same model, so surely someone notices, right?

Some of it has been noticed, after all. The most notorious example is the
rise and fall of the em-dash - a grammatical device that, frankly, I didn't even know
was separate from a hyphen for years, much to the dismay of my English teachers and professors - 
but there have been others. It's not just X, it's Y, lest we forget, and isn't that 
"load-bearing"?  We all know these tics of the models; they are established as common
knowledge.

What isn't common knowledge are the model's defaults. By defaults, I mean the choices
that are so subtle that we don't tend to think of them as choices. Which three
considerations get weighted into those pros and cons you asked for? Which
side of a dispute gets described first and which gets the "however"?
Which company comes to mind as the example of a successful startup?
[Which libraries and languages are the right ones for any given task?](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.17181v4)
All of these read as natural, and each user sees only their own answer. In Naser's phrase,
an individual observes only their own epistemic trajectory (p. 10)[@naser2026sameness].

We finally have our mechanism, and it is a deceptively simple one at that.
To notice that something is a choice, you arguably have to see someone choose differently.
Noticing implicitly relies on contrast, so a default that homogenizes successfully removes
the very contrast that would reveal it. We may notice the tics as distinctive marks, as remnants of
some ["jagged intelligence"](https://karpathy.bearblog.dev/year-in-review-2025/), but we do *not*
give the defaults such attention. They simply look like how the thing is done!^[For instance, one might get
a sense of this by comparing the previous [preprint](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.17181v4) on software development tooling to, say, the Stack Overflow [developer survey](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/) from the same period.] As the writing that surrounds the defaults converges as well,
this result only intensifies.

I was getting at this in my previous work - I did, after all, find the 
similarities between the sparse outputs "staggering" - but our sample size
was rather low. Let's take that example in this application: none of the 30
users that I devised for the experiment would have ever seen the table as I did.
They only would've seen the response to their specific query, read it, made of it what
they would, and, in all likelihood^[Forgive me for assuming a sort of nonchalance here, but I think as mediums become more widespread in use, that sort of nonchalance develops rapidly in the attitude around their adoption.] carried on as if nothing happened.

The strongest objection here comes from Burtch, who looked at competitive data
science on [Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/) and found that syntax converges while approach does not
(p. 7)[@burtch2026hitchhiker]. If this finding holds **in general**, then the model's convergence lives mostly in
the conspicuous layer, the surface, and the substance underneath stays
plural. In this hypothetical scenario the convergence would largely be common knowledge after all:
we *would* all know the code looks alike and the ideas don't.

But Burtch's own reading of his result points the other way. He suggests that what
truly converges is what people delegate (p. 10)[@burtch2026hitchhiker]. A Kaggle
competitor is scored on the approach. It is, after all, the one thing 
they won't hand off, so they choose it themselves and let the model write the
boilerplate. Here we have the Specification Dilemma's density argument turning
up in a new setting: **where people specify densely, outputs diverge.** Let's see where this gets us into trouble.

## Laundered

The case that made this concrete for me is LinkedIn.

Consider, for example, two posts with the same text. The first begins, "I asked ChatGPT
about leadership, and here's what it said." The second excludes the disclaimer, 
presenting the identical words after it as the poster's own hard-won reflection.
Both circulate roughly equally and might even reach the same people.
But the first carries its source with it: you and I read it knowing it is the model,
and we know the other readers know too. The second, in contrast, has had its source stripped.
Circulation isn't the variable, then. It's attribution, a variable I've found notoriously absent on LinkedIn in particular. 
Let's adopt the term "laundered" for the second version, namely, model text circulating as a person's.

The damage of undetected, subtle laundering is that it fakes plurality in a convincing way. If broadcast was one source known
to be one, then laundering is a tricky source that, in a contrived sleight of hand, appears as many sources. If 20 people in my network
post the same insight in 20 slightly different voices, each with a few of their own words, the most natural reading is that multiple people
arrived there independently, giving the facade of consensus. Naser calls this apparent multi-source confirmation
(p. 4)[@naser2026sameness] and connects it to conformist bias (p. 5)[@naser2026sameness]: we give a view more
weight when more people seem to hold it. This is again roughly proportionate; ten posts constitute one source counted
ten times. The evening news, looking pretty harmless now regardless of whatever else it did, never pretended to be your neighbors.

It would be nice, at this point, to say that the detection of laundering can cure us. Unfortunately, it can't, really.

First, detection selects for inconspicuousness. Once readers know the
tics, writers strip them: the dashes go, "delve" and "load-bearing" go shortly thereafter, and so on^[I would really love to do an anonymized trial of, say, University Papers, correlated with a linguistic analysis of what (A) the best available LLMs were and (B) the most widely disseminated LLMs were, over a period of perhaps 2023-2026 incremented by semester. Earlier years before the advent of LLMs could be used as a control. I think we'd see evidence of exactly this; if anyone has done such a study or has access to the anonymized data to do so, please write to me!]. 

Can you guess what survives despite our new reinvention of copyediting here? It's the defaults.
Detection works something like selection pressure on a population. 
It removes the visible traits and leaves the ones you can't see, so the convergence doesn't simply go away;
it would be too reassuring if it did that. It goes deeper.
[Liang and colleagues](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09747), measuring
model-assisted writing across consumer complaints, press releases, and job
postings, saw adoption level off in 2024, and, importantly, allowed that part of the
plateau might be writing that had simply become harder to tell apart from
a person's [@liang2025widespread]. This is an example of what the mechanism here looks like.

Second, detection *removes* common knowledge of minds. Writing used to be
[costly evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signalling_theory) of
thought: if someone wrote a thing, one would generally believe it, at least enough
to give it credence such that spending an evening on it wasn't a huge ask.
That inference was, itself, common knowledge. "I knew you meant it, and you knew I knew."
Once any text might conceivably be the model's, no text proves a human mind behind it.
"Is this AI?" now hangs over every page, and inaccurate "AI detectors" aren't of much help, perhaps
even contributing to the issue with their myriad false positives. Our question here, as a result,
gets asked of laundered text and honest text alike, and the honest writer pays for it too,
adopting some of the corrections invented by pesky launderers trying to avoid detection.

My first instinct about all this? That the model would supersede the
people we talk to, analogous to the way earlier critics feared parasocial ties would
replace mutual ones. The evidence on parasocial ties says otherwise:
Wunderlich finds that they layer on top of mutual relationships rather
than displacing them (p. 50)[@wunderlich2023parasoziale]. I now think the model does something stranger.
It [ventriloquizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ventriloquism) your ties
instead of replacing them.

The same Katz (perhaps the hero of this writing), with Paul Lazarsfeld, gave us the
[two-step flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-step_flow_of_communication):
in short, ideas move from the media to opinion leaders, and from opinion leaders to
the people who trust them [@katz1955personal]. Bennett and Manheim thought targeted media had
collapsed this into a single step [@bennett2006onestep]. Harff and colleagues find, in contrast, the two-step
flow generally upheld (pp. 2351–2352)[@harff2026revisiting], and I think it is they who are right, if you'll allow
an amendment of my own. The second step is still a person you know: the colleague
whose take you trust, the mentor whose email you printed out and still have.
The first step is increasingly the model, and you cannot see it.
Your ties survive, of course, but they carry the foreign words of the model.
It goes for culture as much as for belief, the toast and the eulogy as much as the
opinion.

And one more ominous thing, one that I alluded to last time.
Laundered text gets scraped into the next training corpus as human writing.
This is the feedback loop from the Specification Dilemma with provenance erased:
the last model's defaults enter the next model's data, framed as legitimate evidence
of how human people write. To whatever extent the labs filter their data by detecting model text,
the filter inherits the reader's bias, inherits the false positives that might rule out otherwise quality
text, and we tend towards [model collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_collapse).

## Reprise: What Is Lost

<figure class="prose-excerpt">
<blockquote>
<p>A newspaper then takes up the notion or the feeling that had occurred
simultaneously, but singly, to each of them. All are then immediately
guided towards this beacon; and these wandering minds, which had long
sought each other in darkness, at length meet and unite. The newspaper
brought them together, and the newspaper is still necessary to keep them
united.</p>
</blockquote>
<figcaption>Alexis de Tocqueville — <em>Democracy in America</em>, tr.
Henry Reeve</figcaption>
</figure>

Tocqueville saw it in 1840 [@tocqueville1840democracy]. A thought that occurs to many people 
"simultaneously, but singly" is none other than a convergence. The
newspaper converts this into integration, firing the flare such that the
scattered and lost minds may find each other. The model does the first half of this
work better than any newspaper ever did, and not by a slim margin. It may be able to drop the
same thought into thousands of minds at the same moment, but it cannot light the beacon. Here is what
goes missing.

We lose coordination. I'm not the first to bring up 
[The Emperor's New Clothes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Emperor%27s_New_Clothes), though
maybe Chwe made the point first: 
everyone could see the emperor was naked, and nothing changed until a
child said so where everyone could hear. A million people who have
privately converged on the same view and don't know it are... you guessed it, just a million
people, *not* a public. A distribution simply cannot act in the way that a population can.

We lose contestability. What shared object is there to argue with? 
In the old world one could write to the opinions column, boycott a sponsor,
picket the station, and Gerbner could count. Some default that lives in a
hundred million private conversations has no episode, no schedule,
no archive anyone can sit down and watch. To contest it you must first prove 
it exists, and the evidence is scattered across rooms you can't enter; that seems
an impossible task. Meanwhile, people still argue, and often loudly at that.
Naser describes polarization within a shared distribution
(p. 17)[@naser2026sameness]: two camps fighting in earnest, each drafting its case from the
same priors. I'd prefer to think of it as legitimate jousting instead of
mere fighting.

We lose individuality. In classic
[pluralistic ignorance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluralistic_ignorance),
people privately doubt a norm but assume everyone else accepts it, so the
norm survives everyone's doubt. Prentice and Miller's students, privately
uneasy about campus drinking and each sure they were the only one, are the
standard example [@prentice1993pluralistic]. The model produces a strange mirror image of this: private convergence
believed to be individuality. Each user takes *their* unique essay, *their*
specific plan, *their* take to be chiefly *their* own, and why wouldn't they?
It came out of a private conversation where the other participant is, in all likelihood,
the more intelligent one, about their specific particular situation. What wonder to have the
same entity that can prove [Millennium Prize problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Prize_Problems)
at my fingertips for a casual chat about
my deepest problems! Jokes aside, I'll state a prediction: model users will show
[false uniqueness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_uniqueness_effect),
overestimating how distinctive their model-assisted work is. I conjecture that
their overestimation will grow with how much they delegated. I haven't tested
this, but, in principle, it can be tested: show people their own output alongside
others', and ask how much they overlap. If users judge it accurately, the
thesis would be weaker than I think. But I know [another thesis](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.23674), an analogous one, even at that,
which appears to be weaker than we thought...

We lose our view and notion of the whole.
Only the model provider themself, with access to all of the data, can lay all 30 outputs 
in a table, and so only the provider could even remotely tell everyone what they share.
The integrating sort of view that once collectively belonged to a public is now in private hands, and
open-source won't save us here either for reasons of
decentralization. In terms of my access to a random stranger's chat, whether it's the same entity holding
both of our chats or my computer holding mine and their (private) computer holding theirs, the 
result is the same: I'm generally out of luck.

## Caution!

I'm hypothesizing here, and I'm less certain of what I'm saying
than I was in the Specification Dilemma. In particular, this thesis weakens
to the extent that model text circulates attributed to the model.
In a world where most of it does travel as "ChatGPT says" or similar,
the convergence is visible, and common knowledge comes back
with it. That would *not* invalidate the issues I raised in the previous essay,
but it would mitigate most of this one. I am placing a bet on this not being
the case which is based largely on intuition, both social intuition, yes, but also
my intuition from being someone who works on these models and has seen it from
the inside.

Not all is speculation on this; the ratio of attributed to laundered circulation
is measurable, at least in principle. The measurements so far do not settle it,
but they can guide us: Liang and colleagues estimated that, by late 2024,
roughly 18% of financial consumer complaints, about 24% of corporate press releases,
nearly 10% of job postings at small firms, and 14% of UN press releases
involved model-assisted writing [@liang2025widespread]. The key word is **assisted**, not written whole,
not copy-pasted verbatim. My unverified guess, and it is a guess and nothing more,
is that laundering dominates in proportion to how much status is carried by authorship:
the LinkedIn post might be a common example of this, though I personally find it hard
to believe, people do put such weight on what they write and read there, while the UN press release
is an extreme case. A more extreme personal example might be someone's wedding vows. Do you really
want a "Claude said" there?

I am also hardly the first person to reach many of these conclusions.
Naser got to invisible convergence before I did, along with the previously mentioned
point about polarization within a shared distribution.
Shibanai, Yasuno and Ishiguro, simulating the effects of mass media in
2001, warned that with narrowcasting "the domain of common knowledge might
be reduced" (p. 94)[@shibanai2001global]. (That was 25 years ago, concerning cable.) 

I also finally offer one explicit concession. Strict common knowledge is
asymptotic in a way; it's never actually achieved. Rubinstein's electronic mail game,
a game-theoretic cousin of the
[two generals' problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two_Generals%27_Problem),
shows that two parties confirming by messages that might be lost never get
there, however many confirmations pile up, and that, in his model, they *actually*
behave as though they had no shared knowledge at all [@rubinstein1989email]. So "broadcast gave
us common knowledge" is, of course, an overstatement. Perhaps the real claim is comparative:
*less common knowledge per unit of convergence*. The Specification Dilemma
was about how much content we share; this argument is about the ratio, and the models raise
the convergence without the common knowledge, driving the ratio toward zero.

## Provenance

When Katz worried about segmentation in 1996, his remedy was
institutional: a public broadcaster, an *Integrationsinstanz* in
Holtz-Bacha's terms, whose job it was to keep a shared space open even
when the market would not. Regardless of your thoughts on that answer,
I think we can agree that it fit the problem. The shared channel *had* been a
public thing, and a public institution could hold on to it.

Our new shared channel inherits a contrast, for it is private by construction.
Every conversation with the model is one person and the model, and it should 
probably stay that way, after all. Would *you* want your chats broadcast?
That's what I thought. So what could play the integrating role now?

Attribution is a strong candidate. Text that carries its source lets convergence
be seen. Though I've used it throughout this writing, "ChatGPT says" 
is merely the crudest form of it. Ten posts that say where they came from
read as one source counted ten times, which is, in fact, what they are.
Text that credibly carries a person's name gets back
some of its value as evidence of a mind.

But alas, caution is still warranted, for provenance is not a cure.
It makes convergence visible, but it cannot undo it. A labeled monoculture is
still a monoculture. What visibility buys is the other half of the old
bundle: something to point at together, to argue with, to coordinate
around, or to refuse. It buys integration. Whether it would be
enough, who would build it, and whether anyone would use it when using it
costs them status, I don't know.

## The Promised Ending
Earlier I promised I'd come back to my experiment. So what exactly was it?
My hope was to show that my own writing, in an isolated Obsidian vault, was distinct from
the writing of my agentic vault.^[On my system, the way that these are set up is rather intricate. Agents, for example, are allowed and often directed to read my vault, but never to write to it. This is disallowed by the `AGENTS.md` of my vault (which only says this and directs them to their vault) *and* the `AGENTS.md` of their vault. We also provide a suite of command-line tools for interacting with both vaults: one example is a paper import, as there is a third vault which essentially has replaced [Zotero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zotero) for me; that vault carries only papers, and the tool allows new papers to be fetched from arXiv or other sources automatically and provides hooks for the agents to do a reading pass. A [`systemd`](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systemd) service automatically runs semantic embeddings over the three vaults so that they can be searched both together and individually through the tooling. All of this is simply to say: this is a setup I have thought about **a lot**, and my experiment here is a little less informal than it seems.] 

So what did I do? The vaults, like all Obsidian vaults, were essentially nothing more than fancy collections of markdown files. I devised a strategy that I thought was very clever: train a little [Markov chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chain) type system on each to predict the next word^[You can think of this **very** loosely, if you want, as an LLM if [attention](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.03762) didn't exist.]. I could hand it sentences from my own writings, yes, but more interesting would be to blindly hand it some "prompts" and play a game analogous to the Turing Test that I mentioned earlier: can I *guess* which completion was mine, and which was that of an LLM?

The result was fascinating. I came out ahead, but I attribute this mostly to the fact that the model sometimes got tied up in verbatim passages that I had written, which I immediately recognized. I knew this probably wasn't a meaningful result, and decided to do another comparison. I knew I could use transcripts from agentic sessions in Claude Code, Codex, etc. to identify which agents wrote what parts of the agentic corpus, so the new question was simple: pick two agents in the same family, [Opus 5](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-5) and [Opus 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-opus-5-5), the latter being a newer version of the former. Could Opus 5.5 predict Opus 5's chain better than it could predict my chain? Similarly, what about OpenAI models like [GPT-6 Astra](https://openai.com/index/gpt-6-astra/)?

In all cases, the result was slightly unsettling. Opus 5.5 predicted my chain (43.7%) *better* than Opus 5's chain (42.7%). Similarly, GPT-6 Astra predicted my chain better than it predicted *any* model from Anthropic. What does this mean? I have become something of the population that Wenger's study *didn't* address, as someone who works consistently with LLMs both in personal and professional capacities. My result here is, *perhaps*, the strongest evidence for the thesis I've made over the course of this writing. 

Still, there are a few different reasons that this could be, and I have my guess, but we simply don't know. It *is* possible that I have converged toward the models, and I suspect that I have converged in this way *somewhat*, but not to statistical significance. (I am working on building a portfolio of years of my writings before the year 2023 to test this hypothesis, and will update the page when I have done so.) It is *also* possible that the models converged towards me, and I think this is a much more likely result. This would essentially be a limit of my corpus; since I have chosen only to use my local agentic vault, one must ask *what* is in my `AGENTS.md`? And the answer here is telling: I have a long list (the file is 347 lines long) of house style notes and guidelines that the agents must follow; I am essentially prescribing my writing style to the agents, in a sense. Indeed, I was able to do a secondary analysis of the corpus, and I found that Opus 5.5 generally^[Not all of these are so easy to check, but there are some rules that are quite easy to verify without using an [LLM-as-a-judge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LLM-as-a-Judge) methodology, which I am quite opposed to, and without me needing to read millions of words. The specific analyses I did I'm choosing to keep private, but to give an example of one which I'm not including in future work, em-dashes per thousand words fell from 4.43 for Opus 5 to 0.05 for Opus 5.5.] complied with my rules and guidelines significantly more than Opus 5. I intend to extend this work in a further preprint, and thus defer further linguistic analysis of this sort to that future work.

In a sense, the argument I've made here has turned on me. I am the "provider" with the access that I've earlier argued we don't have in general cases. And consider what if I hadn't had my vaults and this corpus? These facts and observations that I've revealed would've been invisible. I find it both unsettling and reassuring; at least we can now name the phenomenon and I have some sense of what it is like, even if on a more minute scale.

And what will *I* change? Probably not much based on all of this. Even this essay substantially used my vaults, used these features that I have built on. The practice of attribution must be more than an argument, even if [honesty falls short of proof](/essays/swe_vc.html). Yet I still have some hope; even with all of the limitations, and the fact that I deem my ability to predict which chain is mine a result which isn't as meaningful as I'd hope, I think that you may come to the same conclusion that I did. I leave you with two chains, both with the prompt "The point"; it is up to you to guess which is mine and hope you are right.
