Do crawlers react to new website commits?

Commit-response study of the public Forgejo repository neuwirth/levineuwirth.org, October 1 to October 19, 2026 (UTC). Prepared 2026-10-20. All times are UTC.

Short answer

We found no convincing evidence of repeated, prompt fetching after pushes. The dominant user-agent cohort mostly revisited older commits and made no commit-specific GET within an hour of publication. That argues against a crawler that promptly fetched every new commit. It leaves intermittent or otherwise unobserved monitoring unresolved.

The planned timing test is null. The more direct evidence is what was fetched, and when.

  • Planned test: no detected prompt increase. It compared successful GETs in the 30 minutes after each push with the 30 minutes before, over 40 episodes.
    • The raw mean difference was +4.25 requests per episode (829 after, 659 before).
    • Shuffling push times within their hours already produces +1.89 on average, so the rotation-adjusted excess is +2.36 (p = 0.151).
    • Resampling overlapping-window clusters gives a 95% interval for the adjusted excess of −1.47 to +7.75. Resampling whole dates gives −1.97 to +10.47. Neither dependence model is established, and only 10 dates exist.
    • Oct 13 alone produces the excess. Without it, raw +0.55 and adjusted −0.49 (p = 0.71). Its extra requests went to commits more than a week old, not to the new ones.
  • Detection is conditional on the injection model.
    • Planting 10 extra requests in the 10 minutes after every push was detected in 300 of 300 simulations. That model includes days when the cohort was almost absent.
    • Restricted to the 20 difficulty-2 episodes, the same response is detected 68% of the time. On the 15 episodes when the cohort was active (Oct 6–13), it is detected 52% of the time, and +20 requests 96%.
    • These simulations measure added request volume, not hash-specific behavior or every way a monitor could work.
  • The dominant cohort made no commit-specific GET within an hour of publication. This is the linux-chrome-150-155 user-agent cohort.
    • It made 90,122 GETs naming a current commit. The youngest target was 1.35 hours old at the start of its request minute.
    • 36 targeted commits 1 to 6 hours old. 87,270 targeted commits more than 7 days old by the minute midpoint; 87,265 are older even at the start of their minute.
    • Per available commit-hour, it requested commits over 7 days old about 7 times as often as commits 6 to 24 hours old.
    • Apart from four later branch tips, its crawl covered commits published by Oct 3 ~19:00, which it reached 3 to 10 days late.
    • It never requested anything published Oct 3 20:05 to Oct 8 19:36, or anything after Oct 9 20:53, through Oct 19.
    • Its only young fetches were the branch tips of four Oct 8–9 pushes, 1.3 to ~24 hours later. They came on the one day it crawled branch views, which display the current tip. That timing is consistent with link traversal; the logs cannot identify the mechanism.
  • Fast fetches were rare and did not repeat. Requests naming a commit within 60 minutes of its publication came in 4 of 48 study episodes (3 of 43 excluding the Anubis deployment day, which holds E058).
    • Oct 1: a declared crawler was already crawling the repository at about 9 GETs a minute. Twenty minutes after the 11:19 push it burst to about 60 a minute for 40 minutes, fetching that push’s commits among old ones.
      • It ignored the commits of a push made during the burst (11:56), and of every later push before Anubis.
      • It had burst like this seven other times between Sep 27 and Oct 3, four of them about five days after any push.
    • Three other episodes: single browser-like visitors who arrived through links inside the forge.
  • What the evidence supports. On the ladder in § 11, it reaches one temporal coincidence: one crawler burst, once.
    • It does not show a prompt response to publication, preferential fetching of new commits, or repeated monitoring.
    • For the dominant cohort, “fired on each commit” did not happen in these logs. That does not exclude polling that never fetched commits, long or infrequent reactions, other user agents, or activity on another host.
    • None of this identifies who operates any crawler or why it crawls.
  • Revised after review. § 13 lists the corrections.

Coverage (data/coverage.json):

  • Quiet runs. Ten-minute or longer runs with no logged line appeared on Sep 27, Sep 28 02:12–02:38 and Oct 14 19:21–19:33.
  • Rotation gaps. There were three gaps of 60 seconds or more, all after Oct 16.
  • Forgejo upgrade. Oct 1 19:20–19:50.

Owner and internal traffic. Six owner addresses were identified privately, from successful pushes and authenticated pages.

  • Pushes went over HTTPS on most days. No HTTPS push was seen Oct 3–5.
  • Owner traffic is outside the cohort. No request from those addresses falls in the Linux/Chrome cohort. Owner-flagged successful GETs on the repository were few (38, mostly Oct 1–2, other cohorts).

What the cohort looked like. The linux-chrome-150-155 cohort is a user-agent heuristic (X11 Linux x86_64 with Chrome 150 or 155), not a verified identity.

  • Oct 1–5: it was almost absent from this repository, with 0–40 successful GETs a day.
  • From Oct 6: it rose to 3,500–10,400 a day on Oct 9–14, and nearly all of it was commit-pinned file views (src/commit, commits/commit, blame, raw/commit).
  • Git fetches: smart-protocol fetches of this repository by crawlers were essentially zero.

4. Planned primary test

Hypotheses.

  • H0: given the request series, the placement of each occupied UTC hour’s publication episodes is uniform over its 60 rotations, independently across hours (no prompt response).
  • H1: more successful GETs after publication.

Statistic and null.

  • Statistic: T = mean over episodes of (count in minutes +1…+30) − (count in −30…−1). The episode minute is omitted.
  • Outcome: successful (200) GETs of website-repository web pages by the linux-chrome-150-155 cohort, excluding the Git protocol and probes.
  • Null: 10,000 random common circular shifts per occupied date-hour block, seed 20261020.
  • p-value: (1 + #{T* ≥ T}) / 10,001.

Why the p-value is valid. Block rotations form a group, so this p-value is valid under H0 (Hemerik & Goeman 2018; Phipson & Smyth 2010).

  • Hours with one episode. This is exactly interval jitter with 60-minute windows (Amarasingham et al. 2012).
  • Hours with several episodes. The rotation preserves their spacing modulo 60, which is a larger, more conservative null.

What the test does not establish.

  • Not causation. It is not a randomized design, and rejection would not identify a mechanism.
  • Clock-hour pushes. If pushes systematically fell at particular minutes, the synthetic checks show false positives of 18–39%. The observed episode minutes are spread over the hour (8, 12, 16 and 12 per quarter).

Eligibility (A6).

  • Rule. An hour block is usable only if [H − W, H + 60 + W) is fully covered, lies in one stable policy phase and one log regime, and contains no recorded disruption.
  • Applied per block. Eligibility is a block property, so it holds for every shifted placement.
  • Excluded episodes. The rule excludes Oct 4 (5 episodes), E039 (upgrade), E040 (archive switch) and E057 (log cutover).

Ages use the request-minute midpoint (completion times, minute resolution). At the start of the request minute, 87,265 of the cohort’s requests are definitely older than 7 days (87,270 by midpoint).

The scatter of request time against publication time is the clearest view.

  • A monitor would sit on the diagonal. It would place points just above the “just published” line.
  • The cohort forms horizontal bands instead. They sit at commits published Aug 29 to Oct 3, requested from Oct 6 onward. That is consistent with a list of URLs gathered by about Oct 3 and worked through slowly. The logs cannot show how the list was built.
  • Declared crawlers. They crawled steadily until Oct 4, when Anubis denied or challenged them, then stopped.
  • Other user agents (no crawler token in the user agent). Most of their young-commit requests are an Oct 5 wave that re-traversed commits 1 to 7 days old.

Per-episode first-fetch lags are in results/first-fetch-by-episode.csv.

  • Commits up to E053 (Oct 3 18:58). The cohort fetched all of them, but first after 78 to 253 hours.
  • Commits from E054 to E075 and from E080 to E082. The cohort never fetched them.
  • E076–E079. It fetched only the tip commit of each, 1.3 to 7.3 hours after publication (*_tip_first_lag_h), then expanded each tip’s files over the next day. E079’s tip (Oct 9 20:53) is the newest commit it ever requested.

How the tips were found. The cohort requested branch views (src/branch, commits/branch and raw/branch for main, which display the current tip) 218 times on Oct 9. On every other day it made 0 to 6 such requests.

  • Timing. The tip fetches began while that branch-view crawl was under way.
  • Afterwards. Branch-view requests then stopped, and later tips were never fetched, even though the cohort’s traffic peaked Oct 10–14.

This timing is consistent with link traversal that happened to pass through the branch page. It is hard to reconcile with a monitor that fetches each push’s commits promptly. The logs cannot identify the mechanism uniquely, and polling that did not name commits would leave no trace in this analysis.

7. Sources of the fast fetches (private analysis, aggregates only)

Source: source/private_sources.py, run by source/run_private_sources.sh. Output reduced by source/summarize_private_sources.py to results/young-fetch-sources.json. “Sources” are addresses; rotating addresses need not be distinct operators.

Within 60 minutes of publication. 107 GETs from 10 addresses, in 4 episodes; none had an owner signal.

  • E035 (Oct 1). 90 requests from 7 addresses whose user agent declares a crawler token (“shapbot”, a claim).
    • A burst on top of an ongoing crawl. Declared crawlers had been fetching about 9 to 10 pages a minute from this repository all morning. From 11:40 to 12:20 they burst to 19 to 64 a minute, then fell back.
    • New addresses. Most of the addresses doing it were new to the repository after the push. In the same minutes they fetched the push’s 8 commits alongside many old commits.
    • They ignored the next push. E036 (11:56) was pushed during the burst, and none of its 6 commits was ever fetched by a declared crawler. The same holds for the pushes after it before Anubis, except 2 of E037’s 14 commits, 13 days later.
    • Other bursts had no push nearby. Over Sep 27 to Oct 3 declared-crawler rates exceeded three times their typical level 8 times (results/declared-crawler-bursts.json, exploratory). This one was the largest and the only one starting within an hour of a push. Four started about five days after any push.
    • One coincidence, not repetition. This is the single most response-like event in the data. A crawl refresh that grabbed a commit list containing the newest push fits it as well as a trigger does.
  • E033 and E034 (Sep 29, Sep 30; before the study window). Fetches 1.3 to 5 hours later came mainly from browser-like Chrome/Android user agents already crawling the repository, plus single Googlebot-declared requests.
  • E053, E058, E063. One browser-like address each (Chrome/Android, Safari/macOS, Firefox/Linux), arriving through links within the forge.
    • They made 1 to 12 requests, 6 to 11 minutes after a push, mostly to the tip commit.
    • This looks like a person following links. The owner checking their own push is plausible but not established, because these addresses carry no owner signal.

The cohort’s young fetches (E076–E079). 604 requests from 265 addresses.

  • Mostly new addresses. 248 addresses were new to the repository at that moment.
  • A few requests each. The median was 4 repository requests per address in the whole study, with a maximum of 15.
  • The Anubis pattern. Each typically got a 403 challenge, with no referrer, then a 200 whose referrer was the forge (the post-challenge redirect).
  • Only tip commits. No request reached an older commit of those pushes.

8. Alternative explanations checked

  • Author browsing after a push.
    • Owner-flagged traffic is outside the cohort, and removing it changes nothing.
    • Unflagged browser-like visits within minutes of three pushes are possibly the author. They are excluded from nothing, and they are too small to matter.
  • Deploy and sync jobs.
    • make deploy pushes over HTTPS and rsyncs the site over SSH. Neither shows in the web outcome.
    • The 15-minute reconciliation job (from Oct 16) uses SSH on the loopback interface and postdates every push.
    • Maintenance probes are excluded by user agent: 1,357 requests.
  • The time-of-day and daily ramp.
    • Rotation and time-of-day referent nulls both absorb it.
    • The same shape appears on the cohort’s other repositories.
  • Website publication rather than repository monitoring. Each main push is also a website deploy. A crawler watching the website could follow its footer link to the repository root, but the cohort’s successful repository-root requests were zero over the whole study.
  • Feeds, /explore, the user page and the API. All were near zero in push windows.
  • Discovery through GitHub. No request named a commit before Forgejo had it, even when GitHub had the commit up to 17.6 hours earlier.
  • Policy and logging breaks. Episodes are excluded by rules fixed in advance, and the results hold with and without the relaxed variants.
  • Correlation across days. The daily Spearman correlation between pushes and the cohort’s traffic is −0.85 (results/daily-association.json). This is descriptive and confounded: pushes clustered Oct 1–5, before the cohort arrived.

9. Power, robustness and what the null can say

source/power.py: placebo push trains are drawn from the rotation null, a response is planted into the real cohort series in minutes +1…+10, and the planned test is re-run 300 times per size.

What the simulation measures. It is conditional on its injection model.

  • Volume, not behavior. It measures added request volume. It says nothing about hash-specific behavior, and it covers only this one way a monitor could act.
  • Every push gets a response. The headline rows add a response after every eligible push, including days when the cohort was nearly absent. Those zero-background episodes make detection easy.
  • Per-commit sizes are expected counts. “Per novel commit” means an expected number of requests per commit (Poisson), not exactly that many for each new hash.
Injection model (300 simulations each) Detections at α = 0.05
none (placebo false-positive rate) 0.067
all 40 eligible pushes: +2 / +5 / +10 / +20 requests 0.24 / 0.64 / 1.00 (300/300) / 1.00
all 40, expected +0.5 / +1 / +2 per novel commit 0.36 / 0.71 / 0.99
difficulty-2 pushes only (20): +5 / +10 / +20 0.37 / 0.68 / 1.00
cohort-active dates only (Oct 6–13; 15 pushes, 6 dates): +5 / +10 / +20 / +40 0.23 / 0.52 / 0.96 / 1.00

What the counts can and cannot bound.

  • No inferential bound from the zero counts. The cohort fetched nothing within an hour in all 48 study episodes, 43 of them outside the Anubis deployment day.
    • results/power.json keeps zero-event binomial figures: 6.7% for 43 episodes and 18% for the 15 cohort-active ones.
    • Those figures assume independent opportunities with one common probability. Clustered pushes, crawler campaigns, and dates when the cohort was absent do not meet that assumption, so they are an illustration, not a bound on monitoring.
  • What a null p does not prove. Monitoring by a small, quiet actor outside these cohorts cannot be excluded from p > 0.05. Nor can a reaction slower than 3 hours that leaves no age signature.
  • Where the strongest constraint comes from. The age distribution and the never-fetched commits. The timing test adds less.

Synthetic checks (source/test_commitstats.py → results/synthetic-checks.json):

  • Exactness. Under a constructed null with requests held fixed, the rejection rate was 3.3% at α = 0.05.
  • A realistic null. Bursty, day-trending series with a :00-minute cron, and random push minutes, gave 4.5%.
  • Power. A planted response gave power that rises with its size.
  • Failure mode. Pushes fixed at :58 gave 18% false positives, or 39% with a :00 cron. The observed push minutes do not show that pattern.
  • Mechanics. Unit tests cover window indexing, rotation, block eligibility, Holm, Kaplan–Meier and clustering.

10. Limitations

  • The cohort and the clock barely overlap. Before Oct 6 the cohort was absent from this repository; after Oct 13 there were no pushes.
    • So the informative timing data are about 15 episodes on 6 days.
    • Later quiet days are background, not new treated observations.
  • Cohorts are user-agent claims. Address counts are not operator counts. Network and user-agent labels do not identify an operator or its purpose. Nothing here bears on AI training.
  • Policy changes muddy the outcome. Anubis changed who could fetch at all:
    • a 403 challenge is a fetch attempt;
    • in the difficulty-2 phase, successful GETs measure solved challenges;
    • declared crawlers were denied or challenged from Oct 4.
  • Logs see what reached Forgejo.
    • Legacy logs (before Oct 3 22:42:55) cannot attribute non-repository forge routes.
    • Completion times are second-resolution before Oct 14.
    • The start-time variant is JSON-era only.
  • GitHub is outside the logs. Its events API covers only about the last 30 days, and nothing here observes requests to GitHub.
  • The owner flag is incomplete. There were no HTTPS-push signals Oct 3–5, so flagging relies on the adjacent-day rule.
  • The dependence model is unverified. The intervals resample 32 window-overlap clusters or 10 dates. Pushes in separate windows can still share a crawler campaign or a day’s activity, and the two schemes give noticeably different widths.
  • Within-hour exchangeability is assumed, not demonstrated. The synthetic checks validate the mechanics under simulated nulls. They do not show that the real publication schedule is exchangeable within hours.
  • Ages are minute-resolution. Hash ages use the request-minute midpoint.

Prompt response to publication Not observed: 0 of 48 episodes for the dominant cohort within 1 h. The Oct 1 burst is a single occurrence, ignored the next push, and resembles bursts that had no push nearby
Preferential fetching of novel commits Not observed: the cohort requested old commits about 7 times as often per available commit-hour (over 7 d vs 6–24 h), and never reached most new ones
Repeated monitoring behavior Not observed: no actor repeatedly fetched new commits soon after pushes. Intermittent monitoring, polling that never named commits, and activity outside these logs remain unresolved
Identity or intent of any operator Not addressed by these data

12. A prospective design, if wanted

The historical data cannot test a monitor that would fire only when a crawler is active. For a clean test:

  • Fix the outcome and logging plan first. Keep the current JSON logs, add the time_unix and referer fields already present, and use this aggregator unchanged.
  • Publish separated pushes. Make 20 to 30 small pushes, each exposing one new-content commit.
    • Space them at least 3 hours apart, at randomized times drawn in advance, over 2 to 3 weeks.
    • Include quiet control days.
  • Keep everything else constant. Make no make deploy coupling: push without deploying, so website-change and repository-change effects separate. Keep Anubis constant throughout.
  • Choose the primary outcome. Use first GET naming each new commit, by cohort, within 1 hour. Use the randomized schedule as the null.

This requires creating artificial commits, which was not done here and needs the author’s go-ahead.
